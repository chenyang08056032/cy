Opening lead – nine of clubs.

The dealer, when not dealing, exercises no restrictions that enable a good defense pair to score all the tricks to which they contend. Some players ignore these conventions, relying more on instinct than science, but this is a dangerous way to play the game.

Take the present deal, which features top-notch partnership defense. West leads a club, East winning dummy’s queen with the ace. East, disposing the lead as a possible singleton, returns the three of clubs, ruffed by West with the deuce.

East’s return of the three, rather than just any club, is conventional. It says that, as between hearts and diamonds, he would prefer that West lead the lower-ranking suit next. (Had East preferred a heart return, he would have led the eight of clubs to ask for the higher-ranking suit.)

Accordingly, West leads the diamond three to East’s queen and South’s ace. Declarer plays a trump to dummy’s jack and East’s ace, and now East has a genuine problem.

If he thinks West has another trump, East should return a club; but if he thinks West has no more trump, he has three clubs in a diamond return. With only his own 10 cards to rely on, East might easily go wrong and hand South the contract. If he does, however, on the trump, then croak, comes to his rescue.

West’s ruff with the two of spades, followed later by the jack of hearts, is a fine example of three trumps. If West had started with the 6-4-2, for example, he would have ruffed the club at trick two with the jack of hearts. But West had no time to indicate possession of a third trump.

East therefore returns a diamond at trick five, and four spades later, to dummy’s jack, club return. South makes the contract.
