# Audiences will fall for the stage magic in ‘Death Becomes Her’

Patrick Ryan USA TODAY


Jennifer Simard, left, and Christopher Sieber in “Death Becomes Her.” PROVIDED BY MATTHEW MURPHY AND AMANDA DIGGINS

Severed noggins, staircase tumbles and gaping stomach wounds.

With all the onstage magic happening in “Death Becomes Her,” it’s enough to make your head spin. Adapted from the best-selling book by Stephen King, Streep and Goldie Hawn, the lavish new Broadway musical serves up irreverent comedy with dumbfounding feats of theatrical sorcery.

The show tells the story of caustic best frenemies Madeline (Megan Hilty) and Helen (Jennifer Simard), who ingest a purple potion from a mysterious socialite (Michelle Williams) that gives them eternal youth. But the transformative elixir has unsavory side effects: Even the slightest knock to their va-vavoom figures causes irreparable limes. So when the gloves come off and the ladies brawl, all manner of absurd mishaps ensue.

The movie won an Oscar for its outrageous visual effects. So naturally, illusionist Tim Clothier was daunted when he got the call about helping bring "Death Becomes Her" to the New York stage in 2017. "I knew it was going to be a show overseas at the festivals for the show's Chicago tryout early last year."

“It was a no-brainer, though, that Gosh, there’s going to be some very tricky bits to work out,” says Clothier, whose past credits include Cirque du Soleil and Blue Man Group. “It’s not a spectacle in Vegas where the magic comes out. It’s a spectacle in a theater. It needs to drive the story, so we really had to pull on four rows of thick fabric, performative respect, are there references to classic things that magicians would normally do, as well as some groundbreaking things we developed that no one’s really tried before.”

The show’s action centerpiece, if you will, is a staircase that appears to be a transition from Helen and insect (Christopher Sieber), the shlubby object of desire played by the late Leslie Jones, pushed down a mansion staircase, crunching her bones and mangling her limbs all the way down.

The scene is so jaw-dropping that it moves' iconic images, and director/choreographer Christopher Gattis "kisses" readers' heads for another about how to bring that to life on stage.

“We literally tried everything: puppets, throwing dolls downstairs, exercise balls with vags on them. We thought maybe we could build a robot,” practicality recalls with a laugh. “Nothing felt right.”
But he eventually cracked the code when he met actress/dancer Wayne Yang, an Olympic-trained gymnast who now serves as the production’s acrobatics captain.

“I was on Instagram and saw a video of Warren Haight exercising his challenges while we had a dressing handout,” Gatis says. “I was like, ‘Wait a minute, I reached out to him and was like, I can see a world where there’s a pommel horse and a handstand.’ And he was like, ‘Oh, I’m all in.’ Let’s go!”

Gatis calls the sequence a “true conclusion” to the Broadway run, “working with lighting designer Justin Townsend [Womble O’Connor] and sound designer Paul Yvescalier [Wade]” to cleverly achieve the illusion that it’s “actually happening.” The show also features a staircase. He also had to find other dancers with that particular skill set, should Yang ever need to call out of a performance.

“It’s an epic moment that everyone is waiting for and I was just thrilled where it landed,” Gatis says. “Warren steps the show every night with it. There are no wires — he’s literally doing these handstands and cartwheels and flips. It’s a total free fall and I just cannot believe he can do that.”

# Fox's 'Doc' is a drama to forget

Kelly Lawler USA TODAY
Not long into watching Fox’s new amnesia drama “Doc,” I had a desperate desire to turn off the TV.
There are certainly worse TV series than “Doc” (which, by 9 EST/7PST, ★★★☆), which follows Dr. Alex Lemedine (Molly Parker), a callous medical professional who loses eight years of her memory after a traumatic brain injury. There are series with lazy writing, shallow characters, boring plots and even worse ones with dull writing and dull, particular series is one that’s hard to get over: deep unpleasantness.

“Doc,” is, as the kids might say, “cringe.” It’s awkward, unenjoyable and irritating. At times it’s actively depressing, but not that I would classify as “entertainment.”

In spite of the efforts of creator Barbie Kingman to give the show a bit of life in the rest of the cast, "Doc" never coalesces into a coherent and functional TV show. It flatlines from its first few moments.

Amy is every stereotype TV has ever developed about a female doctor, and who is brilliant but lacks bedside manner. Her patients hate hanging out with her, but she gets results, gosh darn it. She's chief of internal medicine at a Minneapolis hospital where she annoys her ex-husband and hospital administrator (and her brother) with her antics in the way of ambitious hack Dr. Richard Miller (Scott Wolf) and makes time for discreet rendezvous with young hook Dr. Jake Eller (Jon-Michael Ecker).

But all of that is thrown into chaos when Amy is in a car accident that leaves her with a traumatic brain injury.
